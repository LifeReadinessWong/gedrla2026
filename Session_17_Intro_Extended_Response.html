Text A — In Support of Community Solar

Editorial — Honolulu Community Review

1Community solar programs — shared solar installations that allow residents to subscribe to a portion of a solar array rather than install panels on their own roofs — represent one of the most promising tools for expanding clean energy access in Hawaii. Unlike rooftop solar, which requires homeowners to own their home, have a suitable roof, and afford significant upfront costs, community solar opens renewable energy to renters, low-income households, and residents of multi-unit buildings who have been largely left out of the solar revolution.

2The financial case is compelling. Subscribers to community solar programs typically see reductions of 10 to 15 percent on their electricity bills, with no installation costs, no maintenance responsibilities, and no long-term equipment commitment. For families in Hawaii who pay some of the highest electricity rates in the nation, even a modest reduction in monthly bills can make a meaningful difference. Studies in states with established community solar programs have found that low-income subscribers save a higher percentage of their income than higher-income subscribers, making community solar one of the more progressive energy policies available.

3Critics argue that community solar programs require government subsidies that could be better spent elsewhere. But the subsidies involved are modest compared to the long-term savings they generate, and the environmental benefits compound over time. Every kilowatt-hour generated by community solar displaces fossil fuel generation, reducing greenhouse gas emissions and Hawaii’s dependence on imported oil. The question is not whether we can afford to invest in community solar. It is whether we can afford not to.

4Community solar also creates local jobs in installation, maintenance, and administration. These are not temporary construction positions but ongoing employment opportunities that keep energy dollars circulating within the local economy rather than flowing to mainland fuel suppliers. Expanding community solar in Hawaii is not simply an energy policy. It is an economic development strategy for communities that have long paid the highest price for energy in the country.

Text B — Concerns About Community Solar

Letter to the Editor — Hawaii Business Journal

1Community solar programs are generating considerable enthusiasm among clean energy advocates, and the instinct behind them — expanding solar access beyond homeowners — is genuinely good. But enthusiasm should not substitute for scrutiny. Before Hawaii expands its community solar commitments significantly, policymakers should take a careful look at the evidence on program costs, subscriber retention, and the actual distribution of benefits.

2The projected savings for subscribers depend heavily on the difference between subscription rates and utility rates over time. In markets where utility rates have been more stable than expected, community solar subscribers have seen savings that were significantly smaller than advertised. In some cases, subscribers locked into multi-year contracts have found themselves paying more than they would have under standard utility rates when electricity prices fell or rates were restructured. The financial benefits are real in the right circumstances, but they are not guaranteed.

3There are also legitimate questions about who actually benefits from community solar subsidies. Research from several states has found that in practice, the majority of subscribers are middle- and upper-income households, not the low-income families that program advocates highlight. Low-income households often face barriers including credit requirements, awareness gaps, and the complexity of enrollment that prevent them from accessing programs nominally designed for them. Subsidizing community solar without addressing these barriers risks creating a program that transfers public funds primarily to households that are already energy-secure.

4None of this means community solar is a bad idea. It means community solar programs need to be designed carefully, evaluated rigorously, and held to the outcomes they promise. Expansion should follow evidence, not just advocacy. Hawaii can lead on community energy — but only if it insists on programs that actually deliver their promised benefits to the households that need them most.

Question 5

★ GED TEST TIP: A strong thesis does three things: takes a clear position (not just "both sides have points"), acknowledges the other side, and points toward the evidence you will use. Write it last — after you have read both texts carefully.
