Clean Water and Community Health

1Access to clean, safe drinking water is one of the most fundamental public health challenges facing communities across the United States today. Because contaminated water can spread diseases including cholera, typhoid fever, and hepatitis A, water quality has a direct and measurable effect on the health of entire populations. As a result, local, state, and federal governments have invested heavily in water treatment infrastructure over the past century — yet significant gaps in access remain.

2The Safe Drinking Water Act of 1974 gave the Environmental Protection Agency authority to set national standards for drinking water quality and to regulate the systems that deliver it to homes and businesses. Because of this legislation, the number of Americans served by water systems that meet federal safety standards has increased substantially. Waterborne disease outbreaks, which were common in the early twentieth century, have become far rarer in areas with functioning municipal water treatment.

3However, rural and low-income communities often face a very different reality. Unlike cities with large tax bases that can fund infrastructure upgrades, rural water systems frequently rely on aging pipes and small treatment facilities that lack resources to meet modern standards. On the other hand, some rural communities have found creative solutions — including regional water cooperatives where multiple towns share the cost of a modern treatment facility, spreading responsibility across a broader base.

4Lead contamination has emerged as a significant concern in older cities. Lead pipes, standard for residential water systems until the 1980s, can leach toxic levels of the metal into drinking water — especially when water chemistry changes or pipes are disturbed during construction. Unlike many waterborne contaminants that cause immediate illness, lead exposure causes gradual neurological damage, with the most serious consequences for children under six. The crisis in Flint, Michigan, demonstrated how quickly a failure of oversight can become a public health emergency.

5The problem of water access is not limited to infrastructure. In many communities, even where water meets federal standards, residents struggle to pay their monthly water bills. Water utility rates have increased faster than inflation, and many low-income households face shutoffs when they fall behind. One solution has been the creation of low-income water assistance programs. Therefore, advocates argue that addressing water access requires both physical infrastructure and financial affordability.

6Climate change is adding new pressure to an already strained system. Droughts are reducing water supply in western states, while flooding in other regions overwhelms treatment facilities and increases contamination risk. Rising temperatures allow algae to bloom in reservoirs, producing toxins that standard treatment processes may not remove. Scientists warn that communities with reliable water access today may face new challenges within decades if investment does not keep pace with a changing climate.

Questions
Question 1
What text structure does paragraph 1 PRIMARILY use?
  • ACompare and contrast — two things placed side by side.
  • BCause and effect — one thing leads to another.
  • CProblem and solution — a problem is named and a fix is proposed.
  • DChronological — events are listed in time order.
Correct: B — ‘Because’ and ‘as a result’ are cause-and-effect signal words. The paragraph explains WHY water quality matters and WHAT HAPPENED as a result.
Question 2
What does the signal word ‘However’ at the start of paragraph 2 tell the reader?
  • AThe author is providing an example to support paragraph 1.
  • BThe author is about to contrast or qualify what was just said.
  • CThe author is continuing the same argument.
  • DThe author is summarizing what has already been stated.
Correct: B — ‘However’ always signals a contrast or qualification. The idea that follows is in tension with what came before.
Question 3
What text structure does paragraph 3 primarily use?
  • AChronological — events in time order.
  • BCompare and contrast — two options side by side.
  • CProblem and solution — a problem is identified and a fix is offered.
  • DCause and effect — one event leads to a consequence.
Correct: C — ‘Problem’ and ‘solution’ appear explicitly. The paragraph names a problem (affordability) and proposes a fix (assistance programs).
Question 4
How does paragraph 2 relate to paragraph 1?
  • AIt provides an example supporting paragraph 1’s main claim.
  • BIt introduces a contrasting situation that complicates paragraph 1’s picture.
  • CIt summarizes what paragraph 1 already stated.
  • DIt provides the cause of the problem described in paragraph 1.
Correct: B — ‘However’ signals that paragraph 2 is complicating the picture — not all communities benefit from the infrastructure investment described in paragraph 1.
★ GED TEST TIP: Signal words are the GED’s road signs. ‘Because’/‘as a result’ = cause/effect. ‘However’/‘on the other hand’ = contrast. ‘Problem/solution’ = problem-solution. Identify the signal word first, then name the structure.
