Grammar 1 — Comma
Which sentence uses a comma correctly after an introductory phrase?
  • AIn her essay the author argues that drilling harms the environment.
  • BIn her essay, the author argues that drilling harms the environment.
  • CThe author argues, that drilling harms the environment.
  • DShe read the article, and took notes on it.
Correct: B — A comma follows an introductory phrase. “In her essay” is the introductory phrase; the comma follows it before the main clause begins.
Grammar 2 — Semicolon
Which sentence uses a semicolon correctly?
  • AThe argument is strong; but the evidence is weak.
  • BShe studied hard; to pass the exam.
  • CText A makes a clear claim; Text B challenges it with new data.
  • DThe author; argues that mining causes harm.
Correct: C — A semicolon joins two related complete sentences. Never use one before a coordinating conjunction (A) or before a phrase (B).
Grammar 3 — Apostrophe (its / it’s)
Which sentence uses the apostrophe correctly?
  • AThe author’s claim is supported by three pieces of evidence.
  • BIt’s impact on the environment has been severe.
  • CThe company lost it’s license after the spill.
  • DBoth author’s agree that drilling is dangerous.
Correct: A — Apostrophe + s shows singular possession. B and C use it’s (= “it is”) where possessive its (no apostrophe) is needed. D needs authors’ (plural possessive).
Grammar 4 — Parallelism
Which sentence uses parallel structure correctly?
  • AThe essay needs a clear thesis, evidence from the text, and you should organize it well.
  • BShe planned to read both texts, taking notes, and then she would write her essay.
  • CGood writing is clear, it is concise, and has correct grammar.
  • DThe essay should include a clear thesis, strong evidence, and organized structure.
Correct: D — All three items are noun phrases: “clear thesis,” “strong evidence,” “organized structure.” The other options mix grammatical forms in the list.

Diamond Mining: Harmful to the Environment

by Tavin McLeavitt

1The global diamond mining industry causes some of the most severe environmental damage of any extractive industry. Finding and removing diamonds requires stripping enormous quantities of soil, rock, and vegetation — permanently altering the landscapes where mining occurs. The industry directly affects an estimated 10 million people worldwide, and while companies and governments have profited enormously, the ecosystems and communities bearing the environmental cost have received far less in return.

2During any large-scale mining operation, the soil, water, and air surrounding a mine become polluted. Open-pit diamond mines can extend hundreds of meters deep and kilometers wide, displacing millions of tons of earth. This removes agricultural land, triggers erosion, and destroys native plant and animal habitats. In Canada’s Northwest Territories, mining companies have drained more than twenty lakes, permanently disrupting the ecosystems of fish, birds, and other species that depend on them.

3Diamond mining also affects water supply and quality in lasting ways. Water is used extensively in the extraction and sorting process. In Africa — where many of the world’s most productive mines are located — fresh water is already a scarce resource. The mining industry’s heavy water demands compete directly with the needs of local communities and agricultural systems that have no alternative source.

4The energy demands of diamond mining contribute significantly to greenhouse gas emissions. Diesel generators and processing facilities in remote locations typically run on fossil fuels and are not subject to the emission standards that apply in more regulated industrial contexts. The long-term environmental impact of these emissions — and of the physical transformation of mining landscapes — is not worth the financial gains that accrue to a handful of corporations and the governments that tax them.

Diamond Mining Companies Find New Ways to Preserve the Environment

by Shaunna Rangel

1The diamond mining industry has a complicated environmental reputation — and in some cases, a deserved one. But a more complete picture reveals that many of the world’s largest producers have made significant, measurable commitments to reducing their impact. The Diavik Diamond Mine in Canada has implemented an environmental advisory board that brings together independent scientists, Indigenous community representatives, and company officials to protect fish populations near the mining area — a program so successful it expanded to include arctic deer.

2Governments in diamond-producing countries have increasingly required rigorous environmental management as a condition of mining licenses. Independent companies now monitor operations, conduct regular audits, and publish findings. Many mines exceed minimum standards, investing in water treatment systems, land rehabilitation programs, and emissions reduction technology not because they are required to, but because environmental performance affects their license to operate and their relationships with surrounding communities.

3Researchers are also making progress on reducing the water and energy intensity of extraction. New separation technologies require less water per carat of diamonds produced, and some operations have shifted to renewable energy sources including solar and wind to power remote facilities. None of this means the industry has solved its environmental challenges — there are operations, particularly small-scale mines in less regulated environments, that continue to cause serious damage. But the trajectory is toward greater transparency, stronger regulation, and more meaningful engagement with affected communities.
