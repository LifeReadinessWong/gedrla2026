Harry S. Truman’s 1947 NAACP Address

1On June 29, 1947, President Harry S. Truman became the first sitting president to address the annual conference of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People. Speaking to ten thousand people gathered at the Lincoln Memorial, Truman delivered a speech that surprised many observers with its directness. He did not merely express sympathy for civil rights — he argued that protecting the rights of all Americans was a moral and constitutional obligation of the federal government.

2Truman opened by declaring that the country had reached a turning point. For the first time in its history, America was truly prepared to guarantee freedom and equality to all citizens — not merely to some. He asserted that every American should be guaranteed a decent home, adequate medical care, worthwhile employment, and the right to a fair trial regardless of race, religion, or national origin. These were not aspirational ideals, he argued. They were obligations.

3Truman did not paint a falsely optimistic picture, however. At the heart of his speech was a sobering account of conditions facing Black Americans in the years after World War II. Returning veterans who had fought for democracy abroad were being denied basic rights at home. Racially motivated violence — including lynchings — continued in the South largely without legal consequence. He cited these facts to make clear why the moment demanded action rather than patience.

4After acknowledging the political difficulty of expanding federal civil rights protections, Truman pointed to concrete steps his administration had already taken — including the appointment of the President’s Committee on Civil Rights, charged with investigating conditions and recommending new legislation. He also cited Eleanor Roosevelt’s leadership of the U.N. Commission on Human Rights as evidence that the country’s commitment to human dignity extended beyond its own borders.

5Truman’s speech marked a significant break from the cautious approach most presidents had taken toward civil rights. By addressing the NAACP directly — speaking to those most affected rather than speaking about them — he signaled that civil rights was a genuine priority. The following year, he issued executive orders desegregating the U.S. military and the federal workforce, becoming the first president to take such direct executive action on race.

6He concluded by invoking words Abraham Lincoln had written in 1862, calling on the nation to remain united in its commitment to human freedom — not only for the present generation, but for all who would come after. The speech is now remembered as an early milestone in the modern civil rights movement, and as evidence that presidential rhetoric, matched with executive action, has the power to shift a nation’s moral direction.

Questions
Question 1
Truman cites Eleanor Roosevelt’s UN work. Which rhetorical technique is this?
  • AAd hominem — attacking an opponent’s character.
  • BFalse cause — claiming one event caused another.
  • CAppeal to authority — using a respected figure to strengthen credibility.
  • DEither/or — presenting only two possible outcomes.
Correct: C — Citing Roosevelt’s international work borrows the authority of a respected figure to reinforce Truman’s own commitment to human rights.
Question 2
The word ‘however’ in paragraph 3 signals a shift. What does the paragraph after ‘however’ do in this argument?
  • AIt contradicts the main point of the speech.
  • BIt introduces problems that Truman uses as evidence for why change is needed.
  • CIt presents the opposing side’s full argument.
  • DIt summarizes what Truman had already said.
Correct: B — After ‘however,’ Truman describes racial violence — not to undermine hope, but to justify the urgency of action.
Question 3
Which of the following would be a logical fallacy if used to argue AGAINST Truman’s civil rights position?
  • ACiting federal statistics on racial violence in post-war America.
  • BArguing that Truman’s proposals would be too costly based on a budget analysis.
  • C‘If we guarantee rights to one group, we’ll have to guarantee everything to everyone — and society will collapse.’
  • DPointing out that civil rights protections were already working in some states.
Correct: C — This is a slippery slope fallacy — assuming an extreme chain of consequences without evidence.
Question 4
Truman invokes Lincoln’s 1862 words at the end. What makes this a rhetorical technique rather than just a historical reference?
  • ALincoln wrote about civil rights, so quoting him proves Truman is right.
  • BIt aligns Truman’s cause with a universally respected figure and appeals to shared national heritage.
  • CIt suggests that Truman’s policies are identical to Lincoln’s.
  • DIt proves that the civil rights movement has been ongoing since 1862.
Correct: B — Invoking Lincoln is a deliberate rhetorical choice — it borrows moral authority and frames Truman’s cause as part of a long American tradition.
★ GED TEST TIP: Logical fallacies feel convincing — that is what makes them dangerous. Ask: does the argument actually PROVE the conclusion, or does it skip a step, attack a person, or use extreme scenarios instead of evidence?
